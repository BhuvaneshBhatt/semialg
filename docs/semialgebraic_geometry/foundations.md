# Algebraic foundations: from equations to geometry

This chapter supplies the algebra behind the real-algebraic algorithms in `semialg`. It is a mathematical introduction, **not** a claim that `semialg` implements every operation discussed here. For implemented operations, consult the [algorithm guide](algorithms.md), [package ownership](../package_ownership.md), and [API reference](../reference/index.md).

## Polynomial equations and their solution sets

For a field \(K\), write \(K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) for the ring of polynomials in \(n\) variables. A family of polynomials \(F\) defines its **zero set**

\[ V_K(F)=\{a\in K^n:f(a)=0\text{ for every }f\in F\}. \]

The subscript matters. The same polynomial may define geometrically different sets over \(\mathbb R\) and \(\mathbb C\):

- \(x^2+y^2-1=0\) gives a circle of real points, but a complex algebraic curve over \(\mathbb C\).
- \(x^2+y^2=0\) has only the origin as a **real** solution, but has one-dimensional complex components \(y=\pm i x\).
- \(x^2+1=0\) has no real roots and two complex roots.

An **algebraic set** is a common zero set of polynomials. A **semialgebraic set** permits inequalities and finite Boolean combinations, for example \(x^2+y^2\le1\) or \((x<0)\lor(x^2+y^2=1)\). Real inequalities are what make ordering, exact signs, and real-root isolation indispensable. See the [introduction](introduction.md).

## Ideals: an algebraic description of the same equations

An **ideal** \(I\subseteq K[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) is closed under addition and under multiplication by any polynomial. The ideal generated by \(f_1,\ldots,f_m\) consists of all combinations

\[ (f_1,\ldots,f_m)=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^m h_jf_j:h_j\in K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\right\}. \]

If all \(f_j\) vanish at a point, every polynomial in the generated ideal vanishes there too. Thus \(V_K(f_1,\ldots,f_m)=V_K((f_1,\ldots,f_m))\): geometry depends on the ideal, not on a particular list of generators.

The **ideal of a set** \(S\subseteq K^n\) is \(I(S)=\{f:f(s)=0\text{ for every }s\in S\}\). The two constructions reverse inclusion:

\[ I\subseteq J\implies V(J)\subseteq V(I),\qquad S\subseteq T\implies I(T)\subseteq I(S). \]

The **coordinate ring** \(K[X]=K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I(X)\) identifies polynomials that agree everywhere on \(X\). It is the algebraic counterpart of treating \(X\) as a geometric object. A nonempty algebraic set is **irreducible** if it cannot be split into two proper algebraic subsets; over an algebraically closed field, this corresponds to a prime ideal.

### Why finitely many equations suffice

The Hilbert Basis Theorem says every ideal in a polynomial ring over a field is finitely generated. Consequently, even if a set is described by an infinite collection of polynomial equations, a finite subcollection of polynomial consequences determines the same zero set. This **Noetherian** property underpins termination arguments for Gröbner-basis algorithms.

It does **not** say that every first-order formula has an inexpensive representation, nor does it bound the size of a Gröbner basis in a practical way.

## Radicals and the difference between real and complex geometry

The **radical** of an ideal is

\[ \sqrt I=\{f:f^m\in I\text{ for some integer }m\ge1\}. \]

For example, \((x^2)\) and \((x)\) have the same zero set; taking the radical removes the distinction between their multiplicities at the set-theoretic level. The **Hilbert Nullstellensatz**, over an algebraically closed field \(K\), states

\[ I(V_K(I))=\sqrt I. \]

Over the reals, the ordinary radical is insufficient: \(x^2+1\) has no real roots even though \((x^2+1)\ne(1)\). The appropriate algebraic replacement uses the **real radical**

\[ \sqrt[\mathrm R]{I} =\{f:\exists m\ge1,\;\exists g_1,\ldots,g_s, \ f^{2m}+g_1^2+\cdots+g_s^2\in I\}. \]

The real Nullstellensatz identifies the ideal of the real zero set with this real radical (for polynomial ideals over a real closed field). A short example makes the contrast concrete:

\[ I=(x^2+y^2)\subset\mathbb R[x,y],\qquad V_{\mathbb R}(I)=\{(0,0)\}. \]

Its ordinary radical is \(I\), since \(x^2+y^2\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb R\), but the real radical is \((x,y)\), precisely the polynomials that vanish at the origin. This is why complex algebraic elimination alone cannot decide real feasibility.

## Gröbner bases: a computational representation of an ideal

A **monomial order** determines the leading monomial \(\mathrm{LM}(f)\) of each nonzero polynomial. A Gröbner basis \(G\) of \(I\) has the property that the leading terms of its elements generate the leading-term ideal of \(I\). Among other consequences, reduction of a polynomial by \(G\) tests ideal membership.

Buchberger's algorithm computes such a basis using **S-polynomials**, which cancel leading terms. The choice of monomial order matters: graded orders are useful for computations, while elimination orders expose relations among a subset of variables.

### Worked elimination example

Consider

\[ f_1=xy-1,\qquad f_2=x-y. \]

Eliminating \(x\), we substitute \(x=y\) to find \(y^2-1=0\). A lexicographic Gröbner basis with \(x>y\) is

\[ G=\{x-y,\ y^2-1\}. \]

Indeed, \(xy-1=y(x-y)+(y^2-1)\), so these two generators define exactly the same ideal. The elimination ideal in \(\mathbb Q[y]\) is \((y^2-1)\), and the real solutions are \((1,1)\) and \((-1,-1)\).

The following computation uses SymPy alone; it illustrates the mathematics without implying an additional `semialg` API:

```python
import sympy as sp

x, y = sp.symbols("x y", real=True)
gb = sp.groebner([x*y - 1, x - y], x, y, order="lex")
assert set(gb.polys) == {sp.Poly(x - y, x, y), sp.Poly(y**2 - 1, x, y)}
```

A crucial warning: a Gröbner elimination ideal describes the **Zariski closure** of a projected algebraic set in general; it need not describe the exact projection of a real solution set. Real quantifier elimination retains the existence and sign conditions that elimination ideals can lose.

## Algebraic dimension versus semialgebraic dimension

For an irreducible complex affine variety, dimension may be defined via chains of prime ideals or the transcendence degree of its function field. For a semialgebraic set \(S\subseteq\mathbb R^n\), its dimension equals the largest dimension of a semialgebraic cell contained in \(S\), and also the dimension of its real Zariski closure. In a CAD, each **sector** contributes one dimension; a **section** contributes none.

Examples:

- a finite nonempty set has dimension 0;
- a circle has dimension 1, although it lives in \(\mathbb R^2\);
- a disk has dimension 2;
- a circle together with one isolated point still has global dimension 1, but the point has local dimension 0.

Dimension is not merely the number of equations subtracted from the number of variables: equations may be dependent, have unexpected common components, or have no real solution. See [real geometry](real_geometry.md) and [topology](topology.md) for further consequences.
